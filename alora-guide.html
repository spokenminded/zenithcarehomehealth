What Alora Is and Why We Use It

Everyone
About 6 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Say in one sentence what Alora is
  • Name the four jobs Alora does for Zenith
  • Explain what EVV means and why it matters
  • Follow the ten golden rules of Alora

Alora is the computer system Zenith uses to run patient care. We use it to plan visits, prove visits happened, write patient records, and send bills. Every team member uses a part of it.

Words to know

Alora
A cloud-based computer system for home health agencies. It includes scheduling, visit proof, patient records, and billing.
Cloud-based
The information is stored on secure computers on the internet, not only on your phone. You can open it from any approved device.
EMR
Electronic Medical Record. The patient's chart, kept on a computer.
EVV
Electronic Visit Verification. Proof that a visit happened.
App
A program you install on your phone or tablet.

Alora does four jobs

Think of Alora as one place that follows every visit from the plan to the payment. It does four jobs, in this order.

  1. 1SchedulePlans who visits which patient, and when.
  2. 2Prove the visitEVV records who, where, and when.
  3. 3Keep the recordNotes, vital signs, orders, and signatures.
  4. 4Get paidBills Medicare, Medicaid, and other payers.

1. Schedule

The scheduler puts each visit on the calendar and assigns it to a team member. You see your visits on your phone. Alora can also tell team members about open visits that need someone. Alora shows schedules from the point of view of the patient, the team member, or the whole agency, by day, week, or month.

2. Prove the visit (EVV)

When you arrive, you clock in. When you finish, you clock out. Alora records the time and the place. This is called EVV. Alora can record your place with your phone's GPS. If you cannot use the app, Alora also offers a telephone method as a backup.

3. Keep the record

After care, you write a visit note. Clinicians also complete assessments and plans of care. Patients and team members sign on the screen. Alora keeps all of this in the patient's electronic record.

4. Get paid

The office uses the visit proof and the notes to bill the payer. If a visit is missing, or a note is not finished, the claim cannot be sent. So your work in steps 2 and 3 pays for the whole agency.

Which app do I use?

Alora has more than one phone app. You may hear the names Alora Anywhere and Alora Plus. The office will tell you which one to install. The name is written in Appendix A.

What you can see depends on your job

Alora gives each person a role. The role decides what you can see and do. An aide sees the schedule and the tasks. The office sees the billing. This protects patient privacy. If you cannot open something that you need for your work, ask your supervisor. Do not ask another person to open it for you with their account.

Why accurate records matter

There is an old saying in health care: "If it is not written, it did not happen." A record is the only proof of the care you gave. It matters for four reasons.

  1. SafetyThe next team member reads your note before caring for the same patient.
  2. The lawMedicare, Medicaid, and Florida require complete records.
  3. PaymentClaims are paid only when the record supports them.
  4. ProtectionA good record protects the patient, you, and Zenith.

The ten golden rules of Alora

If you remember only one page of this guide, remember these ten rules. Every other chapter explains them in more detail.

  1. Use only your own account.Never share your username or password. Never use another person's account.
  2. Clock in and out at the patient's home.Clock in when care begins. Clock out when care ends. Use the real time.
  3. Write the truth.Write what you saw, did, and heard. Never guess. Never copy an old note.
  4. Write it on time.Finish your notes by the Zenith deadline, the end of the same day.
  5. Patients sign for themselves.You can never sign for a patient. You never ask another person to sign for you.
  6. Protect privacy.Open only what you need. Keep screens private. Never share patient information in personal messages or on social media.
  7. Send your work.Make sure everything on your phone is synced before you finish work for the day.
  8. Report problems right away.A late visit, a fall, a missing item, a wrong record. Tell the office at once.
  9. Fix mistakes the right way.Add a correction with the real date and time. Never erase, hide, or backdate.
  10. Ask when you are not sure.A quick question is always better than a wrong record.

Remember

  • Alora plans visits, proves visits, keeps the record, and sends the bill.
  • EVV means Electronic Visit Verification. It is proof that a visit happened.
  • Field staff use the mobile app. The office uses a computer.
  • Your role decides what you can see. Use only your own account.
  • "If it is not written, it did not happen."

Check yourself

What are the four jobs Alora does?

Answer: Schedule visits, prove visits (EVV), keep the patient record, and bill payers (get paid).

What does EVV stand for, and what does it show?

Answer: Electronic Visit Verification. It shows that a visit happened: who gave the service, who received it, which service, the date, where, and the start and end times.

A co-worker forgot their password. Can you let them use your account for one visit?

Answer: No. Every person must use only their own account. Sharing an account breaks privacy rules and makes the visit record false. Tell the office so they can reset the password.

Why does your visit note matter to the billing team?

Answer: Claims are paid only when the record supports the visit. A missing or late note can delay the claim for the whole agency.

The Patient Journey and Who Does What

Everyone
About 5 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Follow one patient from referral to discharge
  • Know which team member does each step
  • See how late work in one step slows every step after it

Every patient follows the same path through Zenith. When you know the path, you can see why your part matters, and who is waiting for your work.

The ten steps of care

  1. Referral

    A doctor, hospital, or family member asks Zenith to care for a patient. The office team receives the request. In Alora, a new patient record is started.

  2. Intake and checks

    The office confirms the patient's details, the insurance, and the doctor's orders. The office also plans the first visit. See Chapter 14.

  3. Initial assessment visit

    A registered nurse (RN) visits the patient. This is the first look at what the patient needs. The nurse also explains the patient's rights.

    Federal rule

    The initial assessment visit must happen within 48 hours of the referral, or within 48 hours of the patient's return home, or on the date the doctor ordered. (42 CFR 484.55)

  4. Start of care and the full assessment

    The date of the first visit that begins care is the start of care. The clinician finishes the full assessment, called OASIS, for Medicare patients.

    Federal rule

    The full assessment must be complete no later than 5 calendar days after the start of care. (42 CFR 484.55)

  5. Plan of care

    The clinician writes the plan of care. It lists the goals, the services, and how often each team member will visit. The doctor signs it. Every visit follows this plan.

  6. Regular visits

    Clinicians and aides visit as the plan says. At each visit you clock in, give care, write a note, collect a signature, and clock out. See Chapters 6 to 12.

  7. Watching and supervising

    The office watches visits on the Live Monitor. A nurse supervises aides. The clinical reviewer checks records for missing items. See Chapters 16 and 17.

  8. Recertification

    Near the end of each 60-day period, the clinician checks the patient again and decides if care should continue.

  9. Billing

    The office bills the payer, using the visit proof, the notes, and the signed orders. See Chapter 18.

  10. Discharge

    When care ends, the clinician completes a discharge record, and the office closes the episode.

Why late work slows everyone

Each step waits for the step before it. One late note can stop the chain.

  1. 1Note is lateThe visit note is not written after the visit.
  2. 2Review waitsThe clinical reviewer cannot check the record.
  3. 3Claim waitsBilling cannot send the claim.
  4. 4Payment is lateZenith receives payment weeks later.

A small delay

A nurse finishes a visit at 3:00 PM but writes the note three days later. The reviewer finds a missing vital sign on day 4. The nurse does not remember the exact number. The claim cannot be completed honestly. Writing the note the same day would have prevented all of this.

Remember

  • A patient moves through ten steps: referral to discharge.
  • The first nurse visit must happen within 48 hours of referral (or the ordered date). The full assessment must be done within 5 calendar days of start of care.
  • The office plans and bills. Clinicians and aides deliver care and write the record.
  • Every step waits for the step before it. On-time notes keep the whole chain moving.

Check yourself

Which team member does the initial assessment visit?

Answer: A registered nurse (RN).

What is the start of care?

Answer: It is the date of the first visit that begins care for the patient. The 5-day assessment deadline and the 60-day periods count from this date.

Who watches visits as they happen?

Answer: The office team, on the Live Monitor. But team members in the field should not wait for the office. Call if you will be late.

Privacy and Security

Everyone
About 6 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Explain what PHI is
  • Protect your Alora account and your phone
  • Know what you may and may not do with patient information
  • Know exactly what to do if a phone is lost or an account is misused

Patients trust us with private information about their health and their homes. A federal law called HIPAA says we must protect it. This chapter shows you how, in simple steps.

Words to know

PHI
Protected Health Information. Any information that points to one patient and relates to health or care.
HIPAA
The federal law that protects patient health information.
Minimum necessary
Look at, use, and share only what you need to do your job.
Username and password
Your username says who you are. Your password proves it is you.

What is PHI?

PHI is any information that can identify a patient and is connected to health or care. If it can point to one patient, treat it as PHI. Here are examples.

Name, address, phone number, and birthday
Social Security number, Medicare number, Medicaid number
Diagnoses, medicines, vital signs, and test results
Visit notes, plans of care, and signatures
Photos and videos of the patient or the home
Phone messages and voice recordings about a patient

Your account is you

Your Alora account is your signature in the computer. Everything done with your account is treated as done by you. So you protect it.

Federal rule

HIPAA requires that each person who uses a health record system has their own unique login. It also requires Zenith to be able to check who opened or changed a record. Act as if every tap is recorded.

Do

  • Use a long password that only you know. A phrase of four or more unrelated words is strong and easy to remember.
  • Lock your phone with a code, fingerprint, or face scan.
  • Log out of Alora on any computer that other people use.
  • Open only the patients you are assigned to, or that your job requires.
  • Keep your phone with you. In a car, do not leave it where people can see it.
  • If Alora asks for a second check, such as a code, follow it every time.

Do not

  • Never share your username or password, even with a supervisor or a friend.
  • Never let another person clock in, write a note, or sign with your account.
  • Never look up a patient out of curiosity, such as a neighbor, a relative, or a public person.
  • Never take photos or screenshots of patient information with your personal camera, unless Zenith tells you to.
  • Never post, text, or chat about a patient on social media or in a personal messaging app. A missing name is not enough. Details can still identify a person.
  • Never leave patient screens open where family, visitors, or other people can read them.

Good practice on public Wi-Fi

When you are not sure a Wi-Fi network is safe, use your phone's mobile data instead. Alora keeps data protected, but a safe connection is better.

Patients, families, and visitors

The patient decides who may receive information about their care. A family member or friend is not allowed to see or hear patient information unless the patient says yes, or the law allows it.

A neighbor or visitor asks you about the patient's health.
Say this

"I cannot share information about patients. Please ask the patient or the family."

Do not

Do not confirm that the person is a patient, and do not share details. Even "yes, I visit that patient every Tuesday" is private information.

If something goes wrong

Mistakes happen. What matters is how fast you tell Zenith. Quick action can stop a small problem from becoming a large one. Report to your supervisor right away if:

  • Your phone or tablet is lost or stolen.
  • You think someone knows your password, or used your account.
  • You sent patient information to the wrong person.
  • You see someone looking at information they should not see.
  • You opened a patient record by mistake.
  1. Call your supervisor now

    Use the phone number in Appendix A. Do not wait until tomorrow. Do not send a text.

  2. Say what happened, and when

    Give the facts: what, when, where, and which patients may be affected. Do not guess and do not hide anything.

  3. Follow the instructions

    The office may lock your account or change your password to keep patients safe. Do what the office tells you.

Report fast and tell the truth

Hiding a problem is always worse than reporting it. A quick, honest report lets Zenith protect the patient and act on time. A late report can make the harm bigger.

Remember

  • PHI is any health information that can identify a patient. Protect all of it.
  • Your account is your signature. Never share it. Never use another person's.
  • Lock your phone. Log out on shared computers. Keep screens private.
  • Never post or message patient information in personal apps or on social media.
  • If a device is lost or an account is misused, call your supervisor right away.

Check yourself

A co-worker says, 'Just clock me in, I am running late.' What do you do?

Answer: Say no. Clocking in for another person creates a false visit record. Tell your co-worker to call the office. If you are asked again, report it to your supervisor.

You want to post a photo of a patient's garden with the caption 'my favorite patient'. Is this allowed?

Answer: No. A photo can identify a patient, and posting about a patient is not allowed. Keep patient information off social media.

You find that your phone is missing after a visit. What is the first thing to do?

Answer: Call your supervisor right away, and tell them what happened. Do not wait to see if you find it.

What does minimum necessary mean?

Answer: Look at, use, and share only the patient information you need to do your job.

Getting Started: Your Account and Your App

Everyone
About 6 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Ask for your account and know what to receive
  • Install the app and allow the right phone settings
  • Log in for the first time and set your own password
  • Do a practice run before your first patient

Before your first visit, you need three things: an account, the app on your phone, and a practice run. This chapter takes you through them one at a time. It takes about 20 minutes.

  1. 1Get your accountThe office creates it for you.
  2. 2Set up your phoneInstall the app. Allow location and notifications.
  3. 3PracticeDo a practice visit with your supervisor.

What you need before you start

  • A phone or tablet that can connect to the internet, with power and enough storage.
  • A data plan or Wi-Fi to install the app.
  • Your username and temporary password from the office.
  • The name of the app your office wants you to use. See Appendix A.
  • The phone numbers for the office and the on-call nurse, also in Appendix A.

Step 1. Get your account

Only the office can create your Alora account. They choose your role. The role decides what you see and what you can do. You will receive:

  • A username (this is your name in Alora).
  • A temporary password (you will change it).
  • The name of the mobile app, and the web address for the computer version, if your job needs it.

Do not

Never use an account made for another person. Never ask for an account in someone else's name. If you did not receive your account, call the office.

Step 2. Install the app

  1. Open the app store on your phone

    On an iPhone, open App Store. On an Android phone, open Google Play.

  2. Search for the app name

    Type the exact name the office gave you, for example Alora Anywhere or Alora Plus. Several apps can have similar names. If you are not sure which one is right, ask the office before you install.

  3. Tap Get or Install

    Wait until the download finishes. Then tap Open.

Step 3. Allow the phone settings Alora needs

The first time you open the app, your phone asks for permission. These permissions make Alora work correctly. Read the screen and choose as shown below.

Also allow notifications. Alora uses them to tell you about schedule changes and open visits.

Battery saver

Battery saver modes can stop GPS and notifications. Turn battery saver off while you work. Charge your phone every night. Keep a car charger with you.

Step 4. Log in for the first time

  1. Type your username and temporary password

    Type them exactly as the office gave them. Watch the capital letters.

  2. Tap Log In

    Tap once and wait. The app may take a few seconds.

  3. Choose your own password

    Alora may ask you to make a new password. Choose a long password that only you know. Do not use a birthday, a phone number, or the word "password."

  4. Look at your schedule

    When you see a list of visits or a calendar, you are in.

Login does not work?

If you cannot log in after two tries, stop. Call the office and ask for a password reset. Many systems lock an account after several wrong tries, and a locked account takes longer to fix.

Step 5. Do a practice run

Never make your first clock in on a real patient by yourself. Ask your supervisor for a practice run. If Zenith has a practice (training) patient in Alora, use it. If not, your supervisor will walk you through your first real visit.

During the practice run, do each of these:

  • Open your schedule and find today's visit.
  • Open the visit and find the address, the time, and the tasks.
  • Find the Clock In button. Do not press it unless you are practicing.
  • Find where to write a visit note.
  • Find where the patient signs.
  • Find the sync button or the sync notice.
  • Find the help or contact information for the office.

If you use a computer

Office team members open Alora in a web browser. Use the web address in Appendix A. Log in with the same kind of username and password. Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark. Do not follow links in emails or messages that ask you to log in, because criminals send false login pages.

  • Clock in and clock out correctly with GPS

Clocking in and out is the most important action you do in Alora. It proves that a visit happened. It must be true, every time. This chapter explains how to do it well.

Words to know

EVV
Electronic Visit Verification. Proof that a visit happened.
GPS
A phone feature that finds where the phone is.

A backup way to clock in and out by calling a number. It is used when the app cannot be used.
Exception
A visit record that does not match the plan, such as a missing clock out.

What EVV records

EVV records six facts about every visit. You give most of them when you clock in and out.

1. The service
What kind of visit: nurse, aide, therapy
2. The patient
Who received the care
3. The team member
Who gave the care. This is you.
4. The date
The day of the visit
5. The place
Where the visit happened

Federal rule

Federal law (the 21st Century Cures Act) requires EVV for Medicaid home health and personal care visits. These are the facts it asks for. The office decides how Zenith's EVV records reach the state system. You do not need to do anything different. You only need to clock in and out correctly.

Follow the same clock in and clock out steps for every visit, unless the office tells you something different.

Alora offers two methods. The app with GPS is the main method. The telephone method is the backup.

    Finish your tasks. Write your visit note. Collect the signatures.

  2. Stay at the home

    Clock out while you are still at the patient's home. Do not clock out later from another place.

    Wait for the confirmation message.

  1. Call the EVV number

  3. Call again when care ends

  4. Tell the office

    Tell the office you used the telephone method, so they can check that the visit is correct.

The rules of clocking in and out

Do

  • Clock in when you are at the home and ready to start care.
  • Clock out when care ends, while still at the home.
  • Use the real time. If you are late, clock in late.
  • Do one visit at a time.
  • Tell the office when something goes wrong, the same day.

Do not

  • Do not clock in before you arrive, or from the car.
  • Do not clock in at the planned time if you arrived later.
  • Do not clock in for another person, or let another person clock in for you.
  • Do not clock in and then leave.
  • Do not change or ask to change times to match the schedule.

Why this matters so much

Clock times are legal records. They are used to bill Medicaid and other payers. A false time, even a small one, can be treated as fraud. It can lead to loss of a license and to penalties for the person and for Zenith. The truth is always the safest choice.

What is an exception?

An exception is a visit record that does not match what was planned. For example, you forgot to clock out, or you clocked in far from the patient's address. Alora flags these visits. The office reviews them before the visit is finished and sent onward.

  1. 1Something is offFor example, no clock out.
  2. 2Alora flags itThe visit goes to a review list.
  3. 3Office asks youYou explain what really happened.
  4. 4Office fixes itThe office records the truth, with a reason.

An exception is not a punishment. It is a check. If you explain it honestly and quickly, it is fixed easily.

    Write what you did, what you saw, what the patient said, and who you called. See below for how to write this well.

  5. Read it once

    Check the numbers and the spelling. Ask: could a stranger understand this?

  6. Save

    Tap Save Note. Wait for the message that the note was saved.

Facts, not opinions

A fact is something you can see, measure, or hear. An opinion is what you think it means. Write facts. You may write your opinion, but mark it clearly and explain the facts that led to it.

Opinion (avoid)Fact (write this)
Patient seems angry.Patient raised voice and said, "I do not want a bath today."
Patient ate poorly.Patient ate about one quarter of lunch (half a sandwich, no soup).
Patient is weak.Patient needed help from two people to stand. Patient sat down after 3 steps.
Wound looks bad.Wound on left heel is 3 cm long, red around the edge, with yellow fluid on the bandage.
Family is rude.Son asked me to leave the room three times. I left and called my supervisor.

A good note and a poor note

Poor note

"Visit done. Patient fine. Nothing new."

Why it is poor: it says nothing. A reader cannot tell what was done, what was seen, or whether the patient was really fine.

Good note

"Arrived 8:02. BP 128/76, pulse 72, temp 98.2, O2 97%. Helped with bath and dressing. Walked with walker to the kitchen, steady, no pain reported. Skin clean and dry. Ate half of breakfast. Patient said, 'I feel tired today.' Told my supervisor at 8:40."

Why it is good: it has numbers, actions, observations, the patient's own words, and who was called.

Poor note

"Patient refused wound care."

Why it is poor: no reason, no time, and no action. A reviewer will ask many questions.

Good note

"Wound care not done. At 8:30 patient refused. I explained that the wound must stay clean. Patient still said no. I did not force it. I called my supervisor at 8:40. A nurse will visit this afternoon."

Why it is good: it says what happened, why, what you said, and what you did next.

Always explain what was not done

If a task on the plan was not done, the note must say why and who you told. Examples of reasons: the patient refused, the patient was sick, the equipment was not available, or the patient was not home. Never leave a task empty and never mark it done if it was not done.

Report changes right away

The note is not the place to report an emergency. If you see a change in the patient, call. For example: new pain, a fall, trouble breathing, confusion, a new wound, a fever, or a very high or low measurement.

Federal rule

The agency must promptly alert the doctor to any change in the patient's condition or needs that suggests the plan of care should be changed. (42 CFR 484.60) Your call to the nurse or supervisor starts this process. Aides call the nurse. Nurses call the doctor.

What never belongs in a note

Do

  • Use the patient's own words, with quotation marks, when they matter.
  • Use only abbreviations that Zenith approves. When in doubt, write the whole word.
  • Write times as you know them, such as "8:40".
  • Write about this visit only.

Do not

  • Do not copy and paste an old note. Every visit needs its own note.
  • Do not write a note before the visit, and do not write what you plan to do.
  • Do not write opinions about the family or other patients. Never use another patient's name.
  • Do not use vague words such as "fine", "normal", or "ok" alone.
  • Do not write anything you did not see or do.

Fix a mistake the right way

Everyone makes mistakes. The right way to fix a mistake keeps the first entry and adds a clear correction. Never hide the mistake.

  1. Do not erase or overwrite

    Do not try to make the old entry disappear, and do not change the date.

  2. Add an addendum or a late entry

    Use the correction or addendum feature in Alora, if it has one. If not, ask your supervisor how to do it.

  3. Say what was wrong and what is right

    Example: "Correction: BP entered as 182/76. Correct reading is 128/76."

  4. Let the system show the real date and time

    The correction must show when it was really made, and who made it.

Late note?

If you forgot to write a note, write it now, and label it as a late entry, with today's real date and time. Do not make it look like it was written on the visit day. Tell your supervisor why it was late.

What happens after you save

Your note goes into the patient's record. The clinical reviewer may read it. If something is missing or unclear, the reviewer may ask you a question. Answer it quickly and honestly, with an addendum if needed. This is normal and helps keep records correct.

Remember

  • A good note is true, complete, clear, on time, and yours.
  • Write facts. Quote the patient. Give numbers.
  • If a task was not done, write why, and who you told.
  • Never copy an old note. Never write before the visit.
  • Fix a mistake with an addendum. Never erase or backdate.

Check yourself

Which is a fact: 'Patient is lazy' or 'Patient stayed in bed and said, I am too tired to get up'?

Answer: The second one. It says what you saw and heard. The first is an opinion.

You wrote the wrong blood pressure in a saved note. How do you fix it?

Answer: Add a correction or addendum. Say what was wrong and what is right. Do not erase the first entry and do not change the date.

It is three days later and you forgot to write a note. What do you do?

Answer: Write it now and mark it as a late entry with today's real date. Tell your supervisor why it was late. Never make it look like it was written on the visit day.

May you copy yesterday's note, because the visit was the same?

Answer: No. Each visit needs its own true note. A copied note can be false. Copied notes are also a known risk in audits.

Signatures and Consent

CliniciansAides
About 5 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Collect a patient signature the right way
  • Know who may sign, and who may never sign
  • Handle a patient who cannot or will not sign
  • Make sure the patient understands what they sign

A signature shows that the patient agrees, or that care was given. It is a legal act. This chapter shows how to collect signatures honestly, in a way that respects the patient.

Words to know

Consent
The patient says yes after understanding what will happen.
Representative
A person the law allows to act for the patient, for example a health care surrogate or a person with power of attorney.
Electronic signature
A signature made on a screen with a finger. It counts as a real signature.

Why we collect signatures

Signatures do two things. They show that the patient received the care at the visit. They also show that the patient agreed to the care. Some payers and programs require a patient signature for each visit. Zenith may also require it. Ask the office which visits need a signature.

Collect a signature step by step

  1. Finish care first

    Ask for the signature at the end of the visit, after care is done. Never ask before care is given.

  2. Explain what they are signing

    Say it in simple words: "This shows that I visited you today and gave you care."

  3. Hand the phone to the patient

    The patient signs in the box with a finger. Give them time. Help them hold the phone steady if they need it, but do not guide their hand.

  4. Check the signature, then accept

    If it is not clear, tap Clear and ask the patient to sign again. Then tap Accept.

  5. Sign yourself

    Your signature confirms that you gave the care and that the record is true. Sign only after you finish.

Who may sign

SituationWhat to do
The patient can sign.The patient signs.
The patient cannot sign, and has a representative who is legally allowed to act for them.The representative signs. Write their name and their relationship to the patient. Ask the office if you are not sure who may sign.
The patient cannot sign, and there is no representative.Use the option for patient cannot sign. Write the reason in the visit note. Tell your supervisor the same day. Follow the instructions you get.
The patient refuses to sign.Do not pressure. Respect the choice. Write that the patient refused, and why if the patient says. Tell your supervisor.

Do

  • Get the signature after care.
  • Give the patient time to read and to ask questions.
  • Use an interpreter or a translated form if the patient does not understand English.
  • Write the reason in the note if there is no signature.

Do not

  • Never sign for a patient, or draw the patient's signature.
  • Never ask another person to sign for the patient unless they are the legal representative.
  • Never get a signature before care, or collect signatures for future visits.
  • Never ask a patient to sign something they cannot read or do not understand.

Understanding matters

Federal rule

Patients have the right to be informed of their rights and of the care in a language and manner they understand. (42 CFR 484.50)

If the patient speaks another language, or has trouble hearing, reading, or seeing, a signature does not mean consent unless the patient understands. Use an interpreter, read the words aloud, or use a form in the patient's language. Tell your supervisor if you need help.

Consent at the start of care

The registered nurse gives the patient important papers at the first visit, such as the notice of patient rights. The nurse explains them and collects signatures. If you are an RN, this is part of your first visit. If you are an aide or another discipline, you may be asked to check that these papers exist before you start. If you are not sure they were done, call the office.

Remember

  • Ask for the signature after care, never before.
  • The patient signs, or a legal representative signs. You never sign for a patient.
  • If a patient cannot or will not sign, write the reason and tell your supervisor.
  • A signature only counts if the patient understands. Use an interpreter when needed.

Check yourself

The patient is tired and says, 'Just sign for me.' May you?

Answer: No. You can never sign for a patient. Use the option for patient cannot sign, write the reason in the note, and tell your supervisor.

When do you ask for the signature: at the start or the end of the visit?

Answer: At the end, after care is finished.

A patient speaks only Spanish. The form is in English. What do you do?

Answer: Do not ask the patient to sign something they cannot understand. Use an interpreter or a form in the patient's language, and tell your supervisor.

For Home Health Aides and CNAs

Aides
About 6 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Use your task list correctly at every visit
  • Notice and report changes in a patient
  • Know what is, and is not, part of your job
  • Understand aide supervision and why it helps you

Aides spend more time with patients than anyone else on the team. You are the eyes and ears of the care team. What you notice, and what you report, can protect a patient's health.

Words to know

Aide
A home health aide (HHA) or certified nursing assistant (CNA).
Care instructions
The written instructions a nurse, or another skilled professional, prepares for the aide. They list what to do for each patient.
Task
One thing the care instructions ask you to do.
Supervisory visit
A visit by a nurse or another skilled professional to check the aide's care.

Your job in Alora

  1. 1Know your visitsRead your schedule. See Chapter 6.
  2. 2Clock in and outAt the home, at the real time. See Chapter 8.
  3. 3Do and mark tasksOnly tasks that are on your list.
  4. 4Write and reportNote what you did and saw. Call if something changes.

Your task list

Each visit has a list of tasks. The tasks come from the care instructions written by a nurse or another skilled professional. Examples: help with bathing, dressing, walking, meals, and moving safely.

  • Do the tasks on the list, in the way the patient wants, when that is safe.
  • Mark a task done only when you did it.
  • If a task was not done, write why. For example: "Patient refused bath. Offered again. Patient said no. Told supervisor at 9:15."
  • Never do a task that is not on the list, unless your supervisor says yes.

Notice and report

You see the patient closely. You often notice small changes first. A small change can be a sign of a big problem.

Trouble breathing, chest pain, or very fast or slow pulse
A fall, or a new injury or bruise
New confusion, sleepiness, or a big change in behavior
A new sore, red skin, swelling, or a change in a wound
Eating or drinking much less, vomiting, or diarrhea
New pain, fever, or feeling much weaker than before
  1. Stay with the patient and keep them safe

    Do not leave the patient alone if they are not safe.

  2. In an emergency, call 911

    Chest pain, trouble breathing, a serious fall, or loss of consciousness are emergencies. Call 911 first. Then call the office.

  3. For any other change, call the nurse or your supervisor now

    Do not wait until the end of the visit, or the end of the day. Use the number in Appendix A.

  4. Write what you saw and who you told

    Write facts, the time, and the name of the person you told.

What is not your job

Every role has limits. The limits protect patients. If a family member asks you to do something outside your task list or your training, say kindly: "I am only allowed to do the tasks on my list. I will call my supervisor." Then make the call.

Do

  • Do the tasks on your list.
  • Tell the nurse what you see.
  • Ask when you are not sure.
  • Treat the patient with respect and keep their dignity.

Do not

  • Do not do tasks outside your list or your training.
  • Do not give medical advice or change what the nurse ordered.
  • Do not borrow money, or accept money or valuable gifts from a patient. Follow the Zenith Code of Conduct.
  • Do not leave before the visit ends, unless the office says you may.

Supervision of aides

Federal rule

A nurse or another skilled professional who is familiar with the patient must supervise aide services. If the patient also receives skilled care, this must happen at least every 14 days. If the patient receives only aide services, the nurse must visit the home at least every 60 days, to watch and assess each aide giving care. (42 CFR 484.80)

A supervisory visit is not a test. It helps the nurse see what you need, such as training, supplies, or clearer instructions. It also protects you, because the nurse sees that you follow the plan. The office tracks when supervisory visits are due. You do not need to ask for one. If a nurse comes while you are working, continue your care as usual.

What happensWhat you do

The patient asks you for a medicine.Do not hand out or decide on medicines beyond what your training and the plan allow. Call your supervisor.
The patient falls during your visit.Keep the patient safe and still. Call 911 if hurt. Call your supervisor. Write what you saw. Fill out an incident report as Zenith instructs.
The patient's family asks for extra help, such as cleaning the whole house.Say you can only do the tasks on your list. Offer to tell your supervisor about the request.

  • Do the tasks on your list. Mark a task done only if you did it.
  • If a task is not done, write why.
  • Report changes right away. Call 911 for an emergency.
  • Stay inside your job. When you are not sure, call.
  • A nurse supervises aide care at least every 14 days (with skilled care) or every 60 days (aide services only).

Answer: Call the nurse or your supervisor now. Do not wait until the end of the day. Write what you saw, the time, and who you told.

A family member asks you to do a task that is not on your list. What do you say?

Answer: "I am only allowed to do the tasks on my list. I will call my supervisor." Then call your supervisor.

How often must a nurse supervise aide care when the patient also receives skilled care?

Answer: At least every 14 days.

For Nurses, Therapists, and Social Workers

Clinicians
About 10 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Follow the timeline from first visit to recertification
  • Finish the assessment (OASIS) and plan of care on time
  • Handle orders, missed visits, and changes in condition correctly
  • Supervise aides and coordinate care

Clinicians carry the clinical record. Your assessments and plans of care decide the care a patient gets, and the payments Zenith receives. This chapter follows the path a clinician takes with one patient. Many deadlines are federal rules, so they are fixed.

Words to know

OASIS
A standard set of questions about the patient's health and abilities. It is part of the comprehensive assessment.
Comprehensive assessment
The full, careful check of the patient's needs. It includes OASIS for patients who need it.
Plan of care
The written plan for one patient. The doctor signs it. All visits follow it.
Certification period
Up to 60 days covered by one plan of care.
Recertification
The new assessment at the end of a 60-day period, to decide if care continues.

The timeline

Step 1. The initial assessment visit (RN)

A registered nurse does the first visit. The nurse checks the patient's immediate needs, explains the patient's rights, and, for Medicare, checks the patient's eligibility for the home health benefit, including homebound status.

Federal rule

The initial assessment visit must happen within 48 hours of the referral, or within 48 hours of the patient's return home, or on the start of care date the doctor ordered. (42 CFR 484.55)

  • You have the doctor's order, and it matches the referral.
  • You explained the patient's rights and gave the written notice before care.
  • You checked the patient's needs, safety, and medicines.
  • You set the start of care date in Alora.

Step 2. The comprehensive assessment and OASIS

After the first visit, the clinician completes the comprehensive assessment. For most adult patients who receive skilled care through Medicare or Medicaid, this includes the OASIS. Patients under 18, patients receiving maternity services, and patients receiving only personal care or housekeeping are not part of OASIS. The office will tell you when OASIS is required.

Federal rule

The comprehensive assessment must be completed no later than 5 calendar days after the start of care. (42 CFR 484.55)

Zenith standard

Zenith target: finish it within 3 calendar days of the start of care date. That leaves time to fix problems before the legal limit.

  1. Answer every OASIS item from what you see and learn

    Answer from what you observe, measure, and ask. Do not guess, and do not copy from an old assessment.

  2. Run the OASIS check in Alora

    Alora can check (validate) OASIS answers and has a tool, sometimes called an analyzer or scrubber, that looks for errors before you submit. Run it. Fix every error and question.

  3. Submit it for review

    The clinical reviewer checks it. The office then sends the OASIS data to the government.

    Federal rule

    The agency must send each completed OASIS to the federal system within 30 days of completing the assessment. (42 CFR 484.45) The office does this. Your job is to complete it accurately and on time.

Step 3. The plan of care

The plan of care says who will give care, what they will do, how often, and the goals. Alora can build the standard CMS-485 from the OASIS answers, and can also offer a "living" plan of care that is easier to update. You review it, correct it, and send it to the doctor.

  • Check that the diagnoses, orders, medicines, goals, and visit frequency are correct.
  • Make sure the doctor's information is correct. Alora can fill in doctor details from the national NPI register.
  • Send it to the doctor. Alora has electronic fax built in.
  • Give care as the plan says. A change in frequency or services needs an order.
Federal rule

The doctor and the agency must review and revise the plan of care as often as the patient's condition requires, and at least once every 60 days, starting from the start of care date. (42 CFR 484.60)

Important

The final claim for Medicare cannot be billed until the doctor has signed and dated the certification and plan of care. So unsigned plans of care stop payment. See Chapter 18.

Orders

  • Written orders: make sure they are in the record and match what you do.
  • Verbal orders: if a doctor gives an order by phone, write it in Alora right away. Read it back to the doctor to check it. The doctor must later sign and date it. Accept verbal orders only if your role and Zenith policy allow it.
  • Face-to-face encounter: for Medicare, the doctor, or an allowed practitioner, must have seen the patient. It must be no more than 90 days before, or up to 30 days after, the start of care, and related to why the patient needs home health. The office collects this. Tell the office if you learn that one is missing.

Visits, missed visits, and changes

  • Visit as ordered. The plan of care sets how often. If you cannot visit, call the office right away (Chapter 6). Write the reason for every missed visit. Tell your supervisor, so the doctor can be told as the plan or policy requires.
  • Report changes. If the patient's condition or needs change in a way that suggests the plan should change, the agency must alert the doctor promptly. Call your supervisor and the doctor.
  • Therapists reassess. At least once every 30 days, for each therapy service given, a qualified therapist (not an assistant) must do the ordered service and reassess the patient's function. The therapist compares the result to earlier measures. The therapist then documents the result and the effect of the therapy.

Step 4. Recertification

Near the end of each 60-day period, the clinician does a new comprehensive assessment to decide whether care should continue.

Federal rule

The recertification assessment must be done in the last 5 days of the 60-day period, which are days 56 to 60. (42 CFR 484.55)

Zenith standard

Zenith target: do it on days 56 to 58. This leaves time to fix problems before day 60.

Alora Plus has a calculator

If you use Alora Plus, it includes a 60 Day Episode Calculator that works without internet. You enter the start of care date, and it shows the dates of the 60-day periods. Ask the office if your app has it.

Supervising aides

A nurse or another skilled professional who knows the patient must supervise aide services: at least every 14 days if the patient also receives skilled care, and at least every 60 days on site if the patient receives only aide services. Write the supervisory visit in Alora, and check that the aide's task list still matches the patient's needs. See Chapter 12.

Discharge

  • Complete the discharge assessment and the discharge summary.
  • Give the patient clear instructions, in a language they understand.
  • Tell the office the discharge date and the reason, so the schedule, the orders, and the billing can be closed.

Notes for therapists and social workers

Therapists and social workers also write visit notes and assessments in Alora. The same rules apply. Write the same day. Write facts. Use the plan of care goals. For therapists, include measurements that show progress. For social workers, include the needs you found, the referrals you made, and the follow-up plan.

Remember

  • First visit within 48 hours of referral (or the ordered date). Full assessment within 5 calendar days of start of care.
  • Run the OASIS check in Alora and fix every error before you submit.
  • The doctor reviews the plan of care at least every 60 days. The final Medicare claim needs the signed plan.
  • Recertify in the last 5 days of each 60-day period (days 56 to 60).
  • Supervise aides every 14 days (with skilled care) or every 60 days (aide services only).
  • Therapists reassess at least every 30 days.

Check yourself

The referral came on Monday at noon. By when must the initial assessment visit be done?

Answer: Within 48 hours of the referral, so by Wednesday at noon, unless the doctor ordered a different start of care date.

The start of care is January 1. What is the latest day to finish the comprehensive assessment?

Answer: No later than 5 calendar days after the start of care, which is January 6. The Zenith target is earlier.

Which days are the recertification window in a 60-day period?

Answer: Days 56 to 60 (the last 5 days).

A doctor gives you an order by phone. What do you do?

Answer: Write it in Alora right away and read it back to the doctor. The doctor must later sign and date it. Only accept verbal orders if your role and Zenith policy allow it.

Intake is the front door of Zenith. Every patient record begins here. A small mistake at intake, such as a wrong digit in an insurance number, can travel through the whole record and stop payment months later. Take your time and check each detail.

A request for Zenith to care for a patient.

The office steps that bring a new patient into Zenith.

The intake steps

  1. Receive the referral and record the exact time

  2. Search before you create

  3. Create the patient record

Review an exception step by step

  1. Open the exception and read what does not match
  2. Ask the team member what really happened

    Ask open questions. Do not suggest an answer.

  3. Check the note and other records

    Does the note agree? Do times in the note match?

  4. Decide

    Accept the record as it is, or correct it to what really happened. If you are not sure, ask the supervisor.

    The reason should answer: what was wrong, how do we know, and who told us.

Poor reason

"Fixed time."

A reviewer cannot tell what was wrong or why the change is true.

Good reason

"Team member forgot to clock out. Team member called at 11:20 and said the visit ended at 10:45. The note is signed at 10:47. Clock out entered as 10:45, as reported by the team member."

It says what happened, how we know, and who reported it.

  • Ask the team member. Write down the answer and the time you asked.
  • Review exceptions within 1 business day.
  • Send serious concerns to the Compliance Officer.

Do not

  • Do not change times to match the schedule, or to make a visit look better.
  • Do not approve an exception you do not believe.
  • Do not let team members tell you what to write to avoid a problem.
  • Do not edit without a reason.
Federal rule

After approval

Approved visits are sent onward, as Zenith has set it up. For Medicaid visits, this means the state EVV system. Some visits can be rejected there, for example because of a mismatch in patient or provider numbers. Check regularly for rejected visits and fix them, using the same honest process.

  • Check the Live Monitor yourself. Do not wait for an alert.
  • Late visit: call, ask if the team member is safe, then start the backup plan on time.
  • If a patient may be in danger, call 911 for a welfare check.
  • Review exceptions by asking what really happened. Record only the truth.
  • Every edit needs a clear reason. Never change times to fit the schedule.

Check yourself

A visit turns yellow. What is your first action?

Answer: Call the team member. Ask first if they are safe, then ask where they are and when they can arrive.

Answer: Do not enter a time you do not believe. Check the note and other records, ask again, and record the real time as best as it can be known. If it cannot be known, record that and the reason, and tell your supervisor.

Answer: It does not say what was wrong, how we know, or who said so. A reviewer could think the change was made to hide something.

Billing and Getting Paid

Office team
About 6 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Follow a claim from the first visit to the payment
  • Send the Notice of Admission on time
  • Check that a claim is ready before you send it
  • Work denials and follow up on money that is late

Billing turns care into payment. Zenith depends on payers to pay for the care it gives. Billing depends on everyone else: the visit must have happened, the record must prove it, and the doctor's signature must be in place. This chapter shows the path.

Words to know

Payer
The company or program that pays for care: Medicare, Medicaid, an insurance plan, or the patient.
Claim
A bill sent to a payer.
PDGM
The way Medicare pays home health. It pays per 30-day period, and groups patients by their needs.
NOA
Notice of Admission. A one-time message to Medicare that a home health admission has started.
Remittance
The payer's report that says what it paid and what it did not.
Denial
When a payer refuses to pay a claim.

The road from visit to payment

  1. Send the Notice of Admission (NOA)

    For Medicare, send the NOA soon after the start of care.

    Federal rule

    The NOA is due within 5 calendar days of the start of care. If it is late, Medicare reduces the payment. The cut is one thirtieth of a 30-day payment for each day that the NOA is late. Those days cannot be billed. A late NOA costs real money.

  2. Care is given and documented

    Every visit has a clock in and out, a signed note, and the patient's signature when needed. This is what your claim rests on.

  3. The assessment is complete

    The OASIS is complete, checked, and sent. Medicare sets the payment group from this information.

  4. The plan of care and certification are signed

    The doctor has signed and dated them, and the face-to-face encounter is on file.

  5. Check the claim is ready

    Use the checklist below. Use any claim checking tools that Zenith's Alora offers.

  6. Send the claim

    Alora can prepare paperless claims for Medicare, Medicaid, other insurance, and private pay.

  7. Post the payment

    Read the remittance. Record what was paid. Mark what was not paid.

  8. Work denials and follow up

    Find out why a claim was denied, fix it, and send it again before the payer's deadline.

How Medicare pays, in short

Under PDGM, Medicare pays home health for each 30-day period. A normal 60-day certification period therefore holds two 30-day billing periods. You saw this in the timeline in Chapter 13.

  1. 160-day periodOne plan of care covers up to 60 days.
  2. 2Two 30-day claimsMedicare pays per 30 days.
  3. 3New periodAfter day 60, a recertification starts a new period.

Other payers have their own rules. Medicaid, managed care plans, and private insurance each have their own forms, approvals, and deadlines. Keep a list of each payer's rules.

Is the claim ready?

Do not send a claim until every answer is yes. A claim sent too early will be denied or, worse, paid incorrectly.

  • The NOA was sent and accepted in time (Medicare).
  • Every visit on the claim has a clock in and out, and the times match the note.
  • Every visit has a signed, complete note.
  • Visits match the plan of care frequency, or the missed visits are explained.
  • The OASIS is complete and sent.
  • The plan of care and certification are signed and dated by the doctor.
  • The face-to-face encounter is on file (Medicare).
  • Insurance is active. Authorization is in place if needed.
  • Name, birth date, and insurance number match the payer's records.
  • Diagnosis codes and dates are correct.
  • For Medicaid, the visits on the claim match the EVV records.
Federal rule

Bill only for care that was given, and that the record proves. Billing for a visit that did not happen, or for a service that was not given, is fraud.

When a claim is denied

  1. Read the reason

    The remittance gives a reason. Write it down.

  2. Find the cause in the record

    Look for the missing signature, the wrong number, or the missing visit.

  3. Fix the cause honestly

    Correct a real mistake. If a required item does not exist, for example a visit that did not happen, do not create or change records to make the claim work. Tell your supervisor.

  4. Send again or appeal before the deadline

    Each payer has a deadline. The office keeps a list.

  5. Learn from it

    Tell the person who can prevent it next time: intake, a clinician, or the scheduler. Repeated denials from one cause show where training is needed.

  • Call the payer about claims with no answer after the usual waiting time.
  • Record each call: date, person, and result.
  • Tell the Administrator about payers that are very slow, or about patterns you notice.

  • Send the NOA within 5 calendar days of the start of care. Late NOAs lose money.
  • Medicare pays per 30-day period. A 60-day period has two.
  • A claim is ready only when the visits, notes, OASIS, signed plan of care, and face-to-face are all in place.
  • Bill only for care that was given and is proven by the record.
  • Fix the real cause of a denial. Never change a record just to get paid.

Safety, Emergencies, and Reporting Abuse

Everyone
About 6 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Decide in seconds if a situation is an emergency
  • Protect your own safety in the field
  • Report an incident the right way
  • Report abuse, neglect, or exploitation, as Florida law requires

Nothing in this guide is more important than this chapter. Patients and team members must be safe. When someone is in danger, you do not wait, you do not ask permission, and you do not look in a manual. You act. Read this chapter now, so you know exactly what to do later.

Is it an emergency?

Is anyone in immediate danger of serious harm or death?
Yes: call 911 first

Examples: not breathing, chest pain, serious fall, heavy bleeding, loss of consciousness, a weapon or violence. Call 911. Stay safe. Stay with the patient if it is safe. Then call the office.

No: call the office

Call your supervisor, or the on-call nurse after hours. Tell them what you see. Follow their instructions. If things get worse, call 911.

When in doubt, call 911

If you are not sure if it is an emergency, it is better to call. You will never be wrong to get help for someone who may be in danger.

Your own safety

Look at the home before you go in. If you feel unsafe, do not enter.
Park in a safe and lit place. Tell the office if the area is unsafe.
Keep your phone charged and with you, so you can call for help.
Leave at once if there is a threat, a weapon, violence, or drug use that puts you at risk.
Use hand washing and the protective equipment you were trained to use.
Report any needle stick, cut, or contact with blood or body fluid to your supervisor right away.

Incidents

An incident is an unexpected event that hurts, or could hurt, a patient or team member. Examples: a fall, a medicine mistake, an injury, a car accident during work, a missing or broken piece of equipment, or a complaint about care.

  1. Make everyone safe

    Care for the patient first. Call 911 if needed.

  2. Call your supervisor

    Do it now, the same day, not later. Say what happened, when, and where.

  3. Write what happened

    Use the incident report form or place that Zenith tells you to use. Ask the office where and how to file it. Write only facts: what you saw, what you did, what the patient said, and who you told.

  4. Do not blame, and do not guess

    An incident report is for learning and safety. Write what you know. Write "I do not know" for what you do not know.

  5. Follow up

    Answer questions. Take part in the review. Tell the office if anything changes.

Patient rights

Every patient has rights. You help protect them every day. The federal rules list them. Here are some of the most important.

Federal rule

Patients have the right to be informed of their rights in a language and manner they understand. (42 CFR 484.50)

To be treated with respect, and to have their person and property respected
To be free from verbal, mental, sexual, and physical abuse, neglect, and stealing of property
To take part in their care, to agree to it, and to refuse it
To receive the services in the plan of care
To have a private clinical record
To make a complaint to Zenith about their care, without fear

If a patient or family member complains to you, listen calmly. Do not argue. Do not promise a result. Tell your supervisor the same day. Every complaint is a chance to make care better.

Abuse, neglect, and exploitation

In Florida, abuse of a vulnerable adult is reported by any person who knows about it, or who has reason to suspect it. A vulnerable adult is a person age 18 or older who has trouble with daily activities or with self-protection. The cause can be a disability, an illness, or the infirmities of age. Many of our patients are vulnerable adults.

Florida law

Any person who knows, or has reasonable cause to suspect, that a vulnerable adult is being abused, neglected, or exploited must report it immediately to the Florida Abuse Hotline. (Section 415.1034, Florida Statutes)

Florida Abuse Hotline

1-800-96-ABUSE (1-800-962-2873)

Open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. You can also use the Florida Relay (7-1-1). Save this number in your phone today.

What to look for

Bruises, burns, cuts, or injuries that cannot be explained
A patient who seems afraid, withdrawn, or who flinches near a person
No food, no heat or water, dirty and unsafe conditions, or no needed care
Missing money, property, or checks. Sudden changes to a will or bank account.
A patient kept from visitors, the phone, or medical care
A patient or other person tells you they are being hurt

What to do

  1. If the patient is in danger now, call 911
  2. Call the Florida Abuse Hotline

    Do this immediately. You do not need proof. You only need a reason to suspect. Give the patient's name and address, what you saw or heard, and who may be responsible.

  3. Tell your supervisor

    Tell them the same day. The report is still your own duty. You do not need permission from the office to call the Hotline.

  4. Write the facts

    Write what you saw and heard, the dates and times, and what you did. Do not write opinions.

Do

  • Stay calm and kind with the patient.
  • Report what you saw, as facts.
  • Protect the patient's privacy. Tell only the people who need to know.

Do not

  • Do not confront the person you suspect.
  • Do not investigate on your own.
  • Do not wait until you are sure. A suspicion is enough to report.
  • Do not keep it secret to protect a co-worker or a family.

Remember

  • If someone may be in danger, call 911 first. Then call the office.
  • Leave any home where you feel unsafe.
  • Report every incident to your supervisor the same day.
  • Florida law: any person must report suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation of a vulnerable adult, immediately, to 1-800-96-ABUSE.
  • You do not need proof or permission to report.

Check yourself

You suspect that a patient's son is taking the patient's money. Do you need proof before you report?

Answer: No. You only need a reason to suspect. Call the Florida Abuse Hotline right away, at 1-800-96-ABUSE, and tell your supervisor the same day.

Do you need your supervisor's permission to call the Abuse Hotline?

Answer: No. The duty to report belongs to you. Tell your supervisor, but do not wait for permission.

A patient falls and cannot get up. What do you do first?

Answer: Keep the patient still and safe. Call 911 if the patient may be seriously hurt. Then call your supervisor. Afterwards, file an incident report as Zenith instructs.

Honest Records and Compliance

Everyone
About 5 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Recognize the records that are never allowed
  • Use a simple four-question test before you record anything
  • Know how to report a concern, and that Zenith protects people who do
  • Understand why honest records protect you

Every chapter of this guide comes back to one idea: the record must be true. Compliance means following the law and the rules. A compliant record is honest, complete, and on time. This chapter explains why it matters so much, and what to do when you are worried.

Words to know

Compliance
Following the law and the rules.
Fraud
Lying or cheating to get money, for example by billing for care that was not given.
Falsify
To make a record say something that is not true.
Backdate
To write a date that is earlier than the real date.

Why it matters

Medicare and Medicaid pay for care. To protect that money, the law says every record must be true. Health care fraud is a serious crime. It can lead to loss of a license, a fine, and prison, for the person who did it and for the agency. Even a small false entry can cause a large problem. But the opposite is also true: an honest record protects you, the patient, and Zenith.

The never list

These actions are never allowed, for any reason, including when a supervisor or a patient asks.

Clock in or out at a time that is not true
Sign for a patient, or sign for another team member
Use another person's login, or let someone use yours
Copy an old note, or write a note for a visit that did not happen
Backdate or change a record to hide a mistake
Record vital signs or tasks that you did not measure or do
Bill for care that was not given
Pay or accept money or gifts for patient referrals

The four-question test

Before you record, or change, anything, ask these four questions. If any answer is no, stop and ask your supervisor.

  1. 1Is it true?Did it really happen this way?
  2. 2Is it complete?Is anything missing that a reader needs?
  3. 3Would I show it?Would I be comfortable if the patient read it?
  4. 4Would it stand up?Would it be fine in an audit years from now?

If you make a mistake

Everyone makes mistakes. A mistake is not fraud. Hiding a mistake can be. So:

  • Tell your supervisor as soon as you notice.
  • Correct it with an addendum or a late entry. Show the real date and time.
  • Never delete, never overwrite, and never backdate.

If you are asked to do something wrong

Sometimes a supervisor, a co-worker, a family, or a patient may ask you to write something that is not true, for example to "fix" a time. The answer is always no, politely and clearly.

You can say: "I am sorry, I cannot do that. I have to record what really happened. Can we find another way to solve the problem?"

Then report it. You are not being disloyal. You are protecting the patient and Zenith.

How to report a concern

Every Zenith team member has a duty to report suspected compliance violations. This includes billing errors, falsified records, kickbacks, and problems with exclusion lists. You may report to:

  • Your supervisor
  • The Administrator
  • The Compliance Officer (the name and phone are in Appendix A)

Zenith's compliance commitments also say:

  • Reports may be made anonymously where an anonymous reporting way is available.
  • Zenith prohibits retaliation, intimidation, or harassment against anyone who reports a concern in good faith, even if the concern turns out to be wrong. If you think someone is punishing you for a report, report that too.

If you believe that reporting inside Zenith is not right for the situation, you may report outside Zenith. These outside contacts are listed in Zenith's compliance documents:

Records are kept, not destroyed

Never delete or destroy a record, a note, a paper, or a message about a patient. The office keeps records for as long as the law requires. If you think something should be removed, ask your supervisor.

Remember

  • Every record must be true, complete, and on time.
  • Never: false times, signing for others, shared logins, copied notes, backdating, made-up measurements, or billing for care not given.
  • Use the four questions: Is it true? Is it complete? Would I show it? Would it stand up?
  • Correct a mistake with an addendum. Never hide it.
  • Report concerns to your supervisor, the Administrator, or the Compliance Officer. Retaliation for a good faith report is not allowed.

Check yourself

Your supervisor asks you to change a clock out time so the visit looks longer. What do you do?

Answer: Say no, politely. Explain that you must record what really happened. Then report the request to the Administrator or the Compliance Officer.

You realize you entered a wrong number in a note last week. What do you do?

Answer: Tell your supervisor, and add a correction with today's real date. Say what was wrong and what is right. A mistake that you fix honestly is not fraud.

Can you report a concern without giving your name?

Answer: Where Zenith has an anonymous way to report, yes. Also, Zenith prohibits retaliation against anyone who reports in good faith.

A

Addendum Also: Late entry
A new note added to a record after the first note was saved. It explains or adds something. It shows the real date and time it was written. The first note is never erased.

The day a patient is accepted for Zenith services. The first visit that starts care is called the start of care.

A state system that collects visit proof (EVV data) from many agencies. Alora can send visit data there.

The computer system Zenith uses to schedule visits, prove visits happened, keep the patient record, and bill. It is cloud-based, which means it runs on the internet.

A careful check of a patient. The clinician asks questions, looks, and measures to learn what care the patient needs.

A block of time for one claim. For Medicare home health it is 30 days.

Caregiver
Any person who gives hands-on care to a patient. In this guide we say team member, clinician, or aide so the meaning is clear.

A block of up to 60 days covered by one plan of care. After 60 days, the patient is checked again (recertification) if care continues.

A bill sent to an insurance company or program for care given.

A licensed health professional who assesses and treats patients: RN, LPN, PT, OT, ST (speech therapist), or MSW (social worker).

Following the law and the rules. A compliant record is honest, complete, and on time.

The patient says yes, after understanding what will happen. Consent is given freely and written in the record.

When a payer refuses to pay a claim. The office can often fix the cause and send the claim again.

The end of care with Zenith. The patient may have met goals, moved, chosen to stop, or need a higher level of care.

Everything written or recorded about care: notes, vital signs, forms, signatures, and orders.

A fax sent from the computer, not from a paper machine. Alora has e-fax built in.

Electronic Medical Record. The patient's chart, kept on a computer instead of paper. Alora is an EMR. Some people say EHR.

Electronic Visit Verification. Proof that a visit happened. It records who gave the service, who got it, what service, the date, where, and the start and end times.

Global Positioning System. Your phone uses it to find where it is. Alora uses it to record where you clock in and out.

The federal law that protects patient health information. It says who may see it and how it must be kept safe.

An unexpected event that hurts, or could hurt, a patient or team member. Examples: a fall, a medicine error, a car accident on the way to a visit.

Scheduler
The office team member who plans visits and assigns them to team members.
ST
Speech Therapist (speech-language pathologist). A clinician who helps patients with speaking, understanding, and swallowing.
Start of care (SOC) Also: SOC
The date of the first visit that begins care for a patient. The assessment and plan of care count days from this date.
Supervisory visit
A visit or check in which a nurse, or another skilled professional, reviews the aide's work with the patient.
Sync
To send the work saved on your phone to Alora, and to get new information from Alora. Short for synchronize.

T

Task
One thing the plan of care asks you to do during a visit, for example help with bathing.
Telephony EVV
A backup way to clock in and out by calling a phone number. It is for times when the app cannot be used.

U

Username
The name that identifies you in Alora. It belongs only to you.

V

Verbal order
An order given by speaking, often by phone. You write it in Alora right away. The doctor signs it later.
Visit
One time a team member goes to a patient's home to give a service.
Visit frequency
How often a discipline visits, written in the plan of care. Example: 2 visits a week for 4 weeks.
Visit note
The written record of one visit: what was done, what was seen, and how the patient was.

Card 3. A good visit note (Chapter 10)

  • Right patient, right date, right visit.
  • Vital signs are real measurements.
  • Every task is marked done or not done, with a reason.

  • I did not copy an old note.

  • Someone may be in danger: 911 first, then the office.
  • Patient problem or change: your supervisor or the on-call nurse, now.
  • Late or cannot make a visit: the office, before the visit if you can.

  • Lost phone or account problem: your supervisor, at once.
  • Suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation: Florida Abuse Hotline, 1-800-96-ABUSE, at once.

  • Every visit has a clock in and a clock out.
  • Every note is saved.
  • Signatures are done, or the reason is written.
  • Everything is synced.
  • Problems were reported.
  • Tomorrow's schedule is checked. The phone is charging.

About This Guide

Everyone
About 3 minutes to read

In this chapter you will learn to

  • Understand where the rules and facts in this guide come from
  • See what the Administrator must check before the guide is final

Purpose

This guide gives every Zenith team member one clear place to learn Alora. It explains the steps, the reasons, and the rules. It does not replace Zenith's policies, your training from your supervisor, or legal advice. When a Zenith policy and this guide are different, follow the Zenith policy, and tell the Administrator so the guide can be fixed.

Where the information comes from

  • Federal rules come from the Medicare Conditions of Participation for home health agencies (Title 42, Part 484, of the Code of Federal Regulations). Sections used: 484.45 (OASIS reporting), 484.50 (patient rights), 484.55 (comprehensive assessment), 484.60 (care planning), and 484.80 (home health aide services).
  • EVV requirements come from the 21st Century Cures Act. Florida's Medicaid EVV program is described by the Agency for Health Care Administration.
  • Abuse reporting comes from Section 415.1034, Florida Statutes, and the Florida Abuse Hotline.
  • Alora's features come from Alora's public product descriptions, for example its EVV, telephony, and home care pages, and the app store listings for Alora Anywhere and Alora Plus.
  • Zenith's own rules come from Zenith's compliance and human resources documents, and from the standards in Appendix A.

The limits of this edition

This edition was written without access to Zenith's own Alora account. Statements about Alora describe features that Alora publishes. The drawings are simple pictures. Screen names and button names in Zenith's Alora can be different. The Administrator should check the items below against the live system before this guide is final.

For the Administrator: check against the live system

  • Which mobile app Zenith uses, and the exact name of the clock in, clock out, sync, note, and signature buttons.
  • The exact words on the permission screens and the login screen.
  • How a team member accepts an open visit (in the app, or by calling the scheduler).
  • How the telephone EVV method works for Zenith, and the number to call.
  • How Zenith's EVV data reaches the state EVV system, and how rejected visits are found.
  • The names of the Live Monitor, the exception review, the OASIS check, and the e-fax screens in Zenith's Alora.
  • Whether Zenith requires a patient signature on every visit, and the correct way to record "patient cannot sign."
  • Where to file an incident report, and Zenith's downtime plan for when Alora is not available.
  • The correction, addendum, and late-entry features in Alora, and what they are called.
  • Zenith's approved list of abbreviations.
  • The Zenith standards in Appendix A, and the phone numbers.

How to update this guide

This guide is one file. You can change text directly in the file. Each Zenith standard appears with a dotted underline in the chapters, and is also listed in Appendix A. Change both places. When rules change, such as a new CMS rule, update the chapter and record the change below.
